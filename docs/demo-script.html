The pitch

ProblemEvery year thousands of good open-source projects die. The maintainer gets a job, burns out or loses interest, and the code freezes. GitHub is full of them: 30,000-star projects nobody has touched in years.

Why nobody revives themThree questions stop people. Am I even allowed to continue this? Why did it die, and will I hit the same wall? If I put in the work, will anyone know it was me?

Our answerDigital Graveyard is a catalogue of dead projects that are legal to revive. We only list a repo if GitHub confirms it's abandoned and its open-source license already allows forking. Each grave gets an autopsy built from its real commit history that says why it died and where to start. Git itself is the proof of work: a revival only counts when your fork has new commits made after you claimed it.

Why it's differentNo mock data, no manual review, no permission emails. The law (the license), the data (GitHub history) and the proof (git commits) are all things nobody can fake. And revivals are community projects: many people can join one grave.

It even tells the next person where to start: 2,600 open issues are a ready-made to-do list.

Prepare the big finish

About 15 minutes the night before. Use a small grave and your own GitHub account.

  1. Sign in as yourself (or a demo account), open a small grave such as clumsy-bird or panelkit, and click Claim & Start Reviving.

  2. Click Fork on GitHub and fork it into your account.

  3. Make one real improvement in your fork after claiming (fix the README install steps, bump a dependency) and commit it.

  4. Back on the grave: Submit My Fork for Verification, paste your fork's URL. It should flip to ✨ Revived.

If you sign in with an account that has GitHub linked, the commit must be made by that GitHub account, so other people can't take credit for your fork.

Questions you'll likely get

Isn't this plagiarism?
No. We only list code whose license explicitly allows copying and modifying it, the original author is credited on the page and in git history, and the original repository is never touched.
Why not just use GitHub's search?
GitHub can't tell you if a repo is dead, legal to revive, or why it died. We combine all three, track who's working on it so people don't duplicate effort, and verify revivals.
How do you stop fake revivals?
The fork must be a real GitHub fork of the original, with commits made after your claim. If your account has GitHub linked, at least one of those commits has to be yours.
What if two people want the same project?
Both join. Open graves are community projects, and the first verified fork becomes the shared one everyone contributes to.
Is any of the data fake?
No. Every grave, score and autopsy comes from GitHub's API. The only made-up things are the six demo accounts used for presenting.
What about projects that aren't open source?
Only their owner can list them, as a donated project. The owner proves ownership with their GitHub account, and the builder puts down a credit stake so they can't ghost.

Data sourceGitHub GraphQL and REST APIs: commits, contributor stats, license, forks, compare
